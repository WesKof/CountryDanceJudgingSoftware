## Usability and plain language

Treat screen space and attention as scarce resources. Give hierarchy, wording, color, shapes and spacing the same deliberate care as a logo or a short advertisement: a quick glance must communicate the intended message. Visual appeal, low effort and confidence affect adoption. Continually evaluate these qualities before, during and after every change, including changes whose primary purpose is technical.

The routine view should answer: What is happening? Who needs to act? What is my next step? If the user is waiting, say so and identify who is next. Keep current actions, actionable blockers and essential status prominent. Supporting scores, calculations, full program details, explanations, history and diagnostic material are additional context; retain them behind clearly labeled, initially collapsed disclosures. Users should not have to read them during normal operation. Do not leave empty visual containers or repeat the same status in several panels. Scope confirmations to the current task/page/stage. Do not carry a prior submission lock message into a newly editable stage; prefer the authoritative current-stage status over a redundant success notice. Verify automatic transitions without reloading, since reloads can hide stale client messages.

Minimize reading, clicks, navigation and unnecessary decisions. Prefer a direct link to the exact missing heat, requested decision, pairing or required form. Task shortcuts must reveal any collapsed ancestors and focus the destination. Keep the competition itself as the user's main focus. Do not add routine gates, confirmations or steps to compensate for unclear design; preserve all necessary scoring, permission, acknowledgment, approval and data-protection rules.

Use concise familiar labels and intentional visual cues. Pair color with text and recognizable shapes or symbols; never rely on color alone. Check actual contrast inside nested panels, including hover/focus/disabled states. Preserve the established cream/forest/gold identity and the user's accepted head-review presentation. Avoid enlarging secondary context to compete with the next action.

Review usability through the actual phases: incomplete setup, scoring, missing scores, pending saves, submitted/waiting, panel decisions, head approval, pending Finals draw and completion. Verify phone and desktop, keyboard access, expansion/focus behavior, and role ownership of actions. Ask whether a first-time user could identify the next step under competition pressure with little reading. Treat observed friction, confusion, excess interaction and poor visual hierarchy as product defects; address reproducible findings proportionately and record material limits in LOCAL_TEST_REPORT.md.

Design for organizers and judges with no technical background or judging experience. Put the next useful action near the top of its section, explain what it does and what happens next, and use short familiar labels. Explain a disabled action beside it with a direct route to fix the blocker. Keep essential names, bibs, placements and approval status visible; place technical identifiers, calculations, full audit history and uncommon management controls in labeled, collapsed details. Group related tasks instead of repeating controls for every record. Do not expose implementation terms such as fingerprints, outbox, CAS or source attestation in everyday instructions. Preserve the detailed records and exact approval/permission checks underneath the simpler interface. Verify the actual control order, status wording, keyboard use and phone layout. Use these instructions for every later feature; PROJECT_SPEC.md records the product contract and LOCAL_TEST_REPORT.md records evidence.
